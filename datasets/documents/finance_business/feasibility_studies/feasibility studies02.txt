A. Program Strategic Context
1. China is a significant contributor to rising global greenhouse gas (GHG)1 emissions that cause climate change
and severely affect the country by their adverse impacts. The country accounts for 27 percent of annual global carbon
dioxide (CO2) and a third of the world’s GHG emissions.2 China is also the world’s largest emitter of methane (CH4), which
has 84–87 times the global warming potential of CO2, over a 20-year timespan.3 It is also the largest contributor of nitrous
oxide (N2O), another highly potent GHG.4 Together, these GHGs contribute significantly to climate change, which causes
rising sea levels, storm surges, coastal flooding, and erosion. These climate change impacts threaten China’s densely
populated low-elevation coastal cities, which account for a fifth of China’s population and a third of its gross domestic
product (GDP). China’s direct annual losses from natural disasters averaged US$76 billion in 2016–2021.5 These impacts
will intensify in the future, and GDP losses are estimated to be from 0.5 percent to 2.3 percent by 2030.6
2. China is committed to transitioning toward a green and low-carbon economy, whereby growth is increasingly
decoupled from resource overuse, carbon emissions, and environmental degradation. To balance green development and
climate objectives, the Chinese government has requested World Bank support to strengthen institutional capacity for
developing low-carbon agriculture and improving soil health. Characteristics of healthy soil include (a) contributing to
climate change mitigation by maintaining or increasing its carbon content (creating carbon sinks); (b) hosting a diverse
community of organisms that help control pests, recycle nutrients, and improve soil structure (high biodiversity); (c)
converting dead and decaying matter into nutrients for plants (high nutrient recycling capacity); and (d) improving its
ability to hold water and nutrients (good soil structure).
3. The Low-Carbon Agriculture and Soil Health Improvement Program-for-Results (PforR Program) in Hubei
Province is framed around dual objectives: (a) to contribute to global public goods (GPGs) by improving environmental
performance indicators such as reducing GHG emissions and increasing carbon sequestration and (b) to enhance the
climate resilience and productive capacity of degraded farmland through soil health improvement. The PforR Program will
achieve this through a suite of innovative sustainable soil management (SSM),7 climate-smart agriculture (CSA), and
agriculture biomass (circular bioeconomy) recycling technologies and practices that are profitable to farmers and farmer
cooperatives and enterprises and replicable at scale. It is the first World Bank-supported operation in China and globally,
focusing on low-carbon agricultural transformation through soil health improvement for healthy food systems.
4. This PforR Program focuses on scaling up low-carbon agriculture approaches to reduce GHG emissions from
intensive crop production systems, through the adoption of CSA and SSM practices and from agricultural waste by
recycling biomass into bioenergy and commercial organic fertilizer (circular bioeconomy) and sequester carbon in soils
(carbon sinks) to improve soil health and productivity of degraded farmlands, as well as enhance ecosystem services.

B. Sectoral (or Multisectoral) and Institutional Context
5. China will remain one of the largest agricultural commodity producers in the world in the foreseeable future.
According to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), the country produced 50 percent of the
global vegetables, 28 percent of rice, 22 percent of maize/corn, 37 percent of fish, 38 percent of pork, 13 percent of
chicken, and 9 percent of beef in 2021. In the same year, China’s agricultural GDP amounted to US$1.37 trillion, equivalent
to 7.3 percent of the national GDP (US$17.7 trillion).
6. China’s agriculture has undergone significant transformation over the past four decades, which has improved
productivity, farmers’ income, and food and nutrition security. However, its high-input and high-output agricultural
production models have proven environmentally unsustainable. China’s agricultural sector now accounts for 13 percent
of the global GHG emissions from agriculture. Agriculture is China's fourth largest source of GHG emissions after the
energy, manufacturing and construction, and industry sectors. Although agriculture, together with land use, land-use
change, and forestry (LULUCF), accounts for only 6 percent of China’s GHG emissions, equivalent to 792 MtCO2-e per year,8
the sector remains key to the country’s GHG emissions reduction efforts. Global climate models estimate that, without
serious national climate mitigation and adaptation efforts, China’s agricultural GHG emissions will rise to 1,350 MtCO2-e
per year by 2050.9 Therefore, reducing GHG emissions from the agricultural sector remains one of China’s top priorities in
its efforts to decarbonize the economy.
7. Several of China’s agricultural practices have historically contributed to farmland degradation. Land degradation
is defined as the loss of productive capacity of land due to loss of soil fertility, soil biodiversity, and degradation of natural
resources which reduces the soil’s ability to provide ecosystem services.10 The major contributors to farmland degradation
are improper and/or overuse of chemical fertilizers and agrochemicals, which increase soil acidity and reduce organic
matter content. In 2022, the total amount of chemical fertilizer used in China was about 50.8 million tons, with average
application intensity of 298.7 kg/ha, compared with the internationally recommended amount of 225 kg/ha. Other factors
contributing to farmland degradation are monocropping, excessive tillage, and removal of vegetation cover resulting in
loss of soil nutrients and organic carbon, which in turn increases CO2 emissions; poorly designed drainage and irrigation
systems; inadequate agricultural waste treatment and disposal systems; and improper disposal of untreated municipal
and industry waste. As a result, 69 percent of China’s total arable land is now of low to medium quality.11 Thus, restoring
the productive capacity of farmlands will enhance food and nutrition security, boost farm income, build climate resilience
of agrifood systems, and increase soil carbon sequestration.12
8. China generates the largest quantity of agricultural waste, including crop straw residues and livestock manure.
Developing innovative technologies that can reduce GHG emissions from agricultural waste has emerged as one of the
top priorities of the Chinese government. Globally, the country ranks first in generating crop straw residues. In 2020,
China’s production of crop straw residues reached 737.5 million tons,13 accounting for 17.3 percent of the global output.
Maize, wheat, rice, and rapeseed are China’s major sources of crop straw residues. Traditionally, these residues were often burned, causing air pollution and emitting CO2 into the atmosphere, or abandoned in or around the fields. In 2019,
the open burning of crop residues in China’s mainland accounted for 17 percent of the global total.14 To mitigate climate
change risks, China aims to recycle crop straw into bioenergy products to reduce CO2 emissions and return residues into
the soil to increase carbon sequestration. China is also the world’s largest producer of livestock manure. From 2007 to
2017, China produced 3.8 billion tons of manure, 18.2 percent of the global output.15 To reduce CH4 emissions, China’s
government aims to treat and recycle 80 percent of livestock and poultry manure annually, by 2025. Estimates suggest
that only around 50–60 percent of livestock manure is currently treated in the country, with large-scale farms having a
higher treatment rate than smaller farms. Recycling treated livestock and poultry manure into commercial organic
fertilizer would further increase soil organic carbon (SOC) and improve soil health and the productive capacity of degraded
farmland in China.
9. Hubei is among the top 10 largest agricultural producers in China. In 2023, the province’s gross agricultural
production value was nearly US$60 billion. The province’s farmland spreads over 5.2 million ha (78.6 million mu),
accounting for 28.2 percent of the province’s total land area. Its total per capita arable land is 0.09 ha (1.3 mu), lower than
the national average of 0.10 ha (1.5 mu). Despite its small farm sizes, Hubei produces 8.4 percent of China’s rice, 6.0
percent of its vegetables, and 10.3 percent of its rapeseed. Hubei is the fourth largest tea producer after Yunnan, Guizhou,
and Sichuan provinces and is a significant producer of fruits.
10. Hubei generates enormous quantities of GHG emissions from its crop production systems. The province uses
more than 2.5 million tons of chemical fertilizer annually, ranking third in China, with an average application rate of 400
kg/ha, 101.3 kg/ha more than the already high national average. Hubei also uses about 52,000 tons of pesticide annually,
contributing to air, water, and soil pollution and adversely impacting food safety and human health. The province also
generates 48 million tons of crop straw residues annually, 27 percent of which are burned in fields, causing air and water
pollution, GHG emissions, and reduced soil carbon stocks. Hubei uses significant quantities of coal in tea processing,
releasing a range of pollutants, including sulfur dioxide, N2O, CO2, volatile organic compounds, ash, and a range of heavy
metals. These pollutants enter the atmosphere, causing damage to the environment and human health. Hubei also
generates about 120 million tons of livestock and poultry manure annually, contributing significantly to CH4 and N2O
emissions. Although about 85 percent of the manure from large farms is treated, not all of it is recycled into other
productive uses or the bioeconomy. This presents an opportunity to use treated livestock manure as a resource to produce
commercial organic fertilizer for use in tea, fruit, and vegetable production systems. This would reduce CH4 and N2O
emissions from the livestock sector and increase soil carbon sequestration.
11. Hubei’s farmland is severely degraded through decades of intensive farming, reducing its productive capacity.
In Hubei, the proportion of farmland suitable for crop production is only 2.3 percent, moderately suitable is 67.7 percent,
barely suitable is 11.5 percent, and unsuitable is 18.5 percent.16 Unsustainable farming practices have depleted SOC. The
average SOC in Hubei Province is 14.2 g/kg, lower than the national average of 17.3 g/kg. Long-term excessive and
improper chemical fertilizer application has increased soil acidification in about 2.0 million ha (30 million mu) of farmland,
lowered soil quality and crop yield, and increased agricultural production costs. The fertilizers used contain trace amounts
of heavy metals, such as arsenic, cadmium, chromium, mercury, and lead, which can accumulate in soils and be absorbed
by crops over time.
12. Hubei Province is highly vulnerable to climate change and is experiencing significant risks and hazards. In recent
years, the province has experienced record heat waves and sudden increases in the frequency and severity of droughts and floods. For example, the severe floods of summer 2020 affected 6.03 million ha of cropland, resulting in crop failure
in the 1.14 million ha in the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River basin,17 of which Hubei Province was the second
most affected province.18
13. Recognizing the growing agricultural emissions and land degradation challenges, China adopted the National
Agricultural Sustainable Development Plan (ASDP, 2015–2030). The ASDP includes measures to reduce the use of
chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and agricultural plastics and to recycle agricultural waste (crop straw residues and livestock
and poultry manure) into biomass energy (for example, biochar, briquettes, and biogas) and commercial organic fertilizer.
These measures aim to help China reduce GHG emissions and increase soil carbon sequestration to improve soil health
and productivity while enhancing climate resilience
